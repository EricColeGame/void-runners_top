If you have seen the name Void Runners floating around and are not sure what it refers to, the short answer is this: it is an unreleased co-op twin-stick roguelike from solo developer Aaron Reisman, listed on Steam as app 5334520 with a release window of Q1 2027. It is not Solar Ash, Void Crew, Void Stranger, or Starfinder, despite the shared vocabulary. The [official Steam page](https://store.steampowered.com/app/5334520/) is the only place where the game's identity and features are documented, and it is unusually terse.

That terseness matters. Void Runners is a game whose entire pitch rests on one unusual mechanic, and the store description explains that mechanic without explaining much around it. What follows separates what the developer actually advertises from what the community has inferred, and from what nobody knows yet.

## What the Store Page Actually Says

The verified Steam listing for app 5334520 describes co-op salvage raids for one to four pilots, playable on one couch or online. Each pair of ships is joined by a beam. That beam shreds anything it sweeps. Crew members spread out, swing the web of beams around the play space, and catch enemies in crossfire while dodging and reviving teammates from escape pods. Between fights, you build a salvaged ship. A run crosses three sectors of four jumps each and ends at a guardian, with three guardians in total.

Developer and publisher are both listed as Aaron Reisman. The release date is Q1 2027, and the page is marked coming soon as of the retrieval date. That is the complete body of confirmed information.

Notice what is absent. There are no ship parts, weapon stats, boss names, sector layouts, unlock costs, or code system published anywhere on the store page. If you encounter a guide that lists a specific beam damage value or a guardian called something specific, that guide is either guessing or working from information the developer has not released.

## Why the Beam Changes Everything

Most twin-stick shooters treat positioning as a personal problem. You dodge for yourself, you aim for yourself, and your teammates are largely independent actors who happen to share a health bar economy. Void Runners inverts that. Because a beam connects two ships, your position is never only your own business. Where you fly determines what your partner's beam sweeps, and their movement determines yours.

That produces a few practical consequences worth thinking through before the game launches:

- **Spacing is a shared resource.** Spread too far and the beam stretches thin across the arena, potentially sweeping a wide but unwieldy arc. Cluster too tightly and you sweep almost nothing. The store description explicitly frames spreading out and swinging the web as the intended play pattern.
- **Crossfire is the payoff.** Two beams crossing means enemies caught between them take damage from multiple angles. That is a positioning problem, not an aim problem, which is why the description leads with movement rather than shooting.
- **Downed players are not gone.** Reviving crew from escape pods keeps a wiped pilot in the run rather than ending it, which softens the usual roguelike punishment for one player dying early.

## Couch Co-op, Online, and One to Four Pilots

The listing supports one to four players, and notably supports both same-couch and online play. That dual support is worth flagging because it is not universal in the genre. A beam-based game is fundamentally a coordination game, and couch play removes voice-chat latency from the equation entirely, which may make it the more natural fit for learning how your partner moves.

Playing solo is listed as supported, which raises an obvious question the store page does not answer: how a beam works when there is no second ship to connect to. Whether solo play removes the beam, links you to a drone, or links two ships under one player's control is not documented. Anyone claiming to know is speculating.

## Three Sectors, Four Jumps, Three Guardians

The advertised run structure is compact. Three sectors, four jumps per sector, and a guardian at the end of each sector. That reads as a deliberately short run length, closer to a tight arcade session than a sprawling campaign, which fits a roguelike where a single mistake can unravel a beam web.

What lives inside those sectors is not public. Enemy types, hazards, jump selection, and whether sectors have distinct visual identities are all undocumented. The official page is the place to watch, since a coming-soon listing typically gains screenshots, trailers, and expanded text as release approaches.

## What Third-Party Guides Can and Cannot Tell You

Void Runners has attracted attention from aggregator sites and guide writers, largely because the beam mechanic is novel and easy to write about speculatively. Treat those sources carefully. A third-party guide can legitimately describe what a trailer shows or interpret the store text. It cannot legitimately report beam damage numbers, item drop rates, guardian health pools, or unlock costs, because none of that has been published.

The practical test is simple: if a claim cannot be traced back to the [Steam listing](https://store.steampowered.com/app/5334520/), a developer post, or observable footage, assume it is inferred. When the game ships, the in-game menus, the ship-building screen, and the run summary will be the authoritative source for any number a guide claims.

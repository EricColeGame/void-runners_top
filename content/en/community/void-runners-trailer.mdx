"Void Runners" is an easy name to confuse with something else. It is not Solar Ash. It is not Void Crew, Void Stranger, or Starfinder. It is a separate project from developer and publisher Aaron Reisman, listed on Steam as app 5334520 and marked "coming soon" with a Q1 2027 release window. If you have seen the name floating around and assumed it belonged to one of those other titles, that assumption is the first thing worth clearing up. The second thing worth clearing up is what the game actually asks you to do, because the pitch is unusually specific: your ship is not a solo unit. It is half of a pair, tethered by a beam.

## The Core Idea: Two Ships, One Beam

According to the official [Steam store page](https://store.steampowered.com/app/5334520/), Void Runners is a twin-stick roguelike for one to four players, playable on one couch or online. Ships are joined in pairs by a beam. Anything that beam sweeps gets shredded.

That single sentence reshapes how you move. In a normal twin-stick shooter, your position is about you: where you are safe, where your guns point, where the next threat comes from. In Void Runners, your position is also about your partner, because the line between you is a weapon. Flying apart stretches the beam across more of the screen. Flying together bunches it into something closer to a point. Neither is automatically correct. The store description frames the whole thing as spreading out, swinging the web, and catching enemies in crossfire, which tells you the designers expect the beam to be used as an area tool rather than a static link.

For a solo player, this raises an obvious question the page does not answer: whether one pilot controls both ships, or whether the beam works differently alone. That is not documented. The honest answer is that the store page advertises one-to-four-player support without explaining the solo configuration, and anyone interested should watch the official page for clarification rather than assume a control scheme.

## Run Structure: Three Sectors, Four Jumps, One Guardian

The description states that a run crosses three sectors of four jumps, ending at a guardian. That is a clean, legible shape: twelve jumps plus three guardian encounters, assuming the pattern holds across all three sectors. It also explains the roguelike framing. A run is short enough to finish in a sitting, which matters for a couch co-op game where four people have to agree on when to stop.

What the page does not say is what happens in a jump, how difficulty scales between sectors, whether guardians differ mechanically, or what a failed run costs you. Those are the questions a player will want answered before buying, and right now the only reliable sources are the official store page and whatever the developer posts directly. Third-party coverage may speculate, but speculation is not documentation.

## Co-op Logistics and the Salvage Loop

The most interesting design pressure here is social. Because beams connect ships in pairs, a four-player group is really two beam-pairs flying in formation. That creates a coordination problem the game seems built around: your beam wants space to sweep, but your crew wants crossfire, and those two goals pull in different directions.

Reviving from escape pods softens the punishment for splitting up. Instead of a downed player being dead weight, there is apparently a recovery step, which encourages aggressive positioning without making one mistake fatal.

Ship building is the other advertised pillar, described as building a salvaged ship. Salvage is the stated resource, but what you spend it on, how many options exist, and whether choices are permanent within a run are all undocumented. If you want to know before launch, the Steam page is the place that will update first.

Most roguelikes ask you to keep yourself alive. Void Runners, an upcoming twin-stick roguelike from solo developer and publisher Aaron Reisman, asks you to keep the whole formation alive at once. The hook is simple to state and hard to play: every pair of ships is joined by a beam, and that beam shreds anything it sweeps. Your survival depends less on your individual aim than on where you fly relative to your crew.

That single constraint reshapes almost every decision in the game, from how you spread out to how you revive a downed teammate. According to the official Steam page for app 5334520, the game was marked "coming soon" with a Q1 2027 release window at the time of writing. That page is the only authoritative source of feature information right now, and it is deliberately short on specifics. This article sticks to what the store page confirms, flags what the community has observed, and names plainly what is not documented.

## What the Beam Mechanic Actually Changes

In a conventional twin-stick shooter, your ship is a self-contained weapon. You point, you fire, you reposition for your own benefit. Void Runners breaks that assumption. The store description says beams link ships and shred anything they sweep, so your damage output is a shared, moving object rather than a projectile you own.

The practical consequences follow directly from that description:

- **Spacing is the primary skill.** Flying apart stretches the beam over more ground but thins your coverage; staying tight concentrates it but clusters your crew into a single target.
- **Movement is damage.** The description emphasizes "swinging the web," which means you destroy things by repositioning, not just by holding a trigger.
- **Crossfire matters.** With multiple linked ships, the geometry between pilots creates overlapping kill zones that no single ship could produce.
- **Dodging is a group problem.** When you swing to sweep an enemy, you also move the beam that keeps you connected.

Because two ships share one beam, a run rewards pilots who communicate about *where* they are, not just *what* they are shooting. That is the entire game's identity in one sentence, and it is the part of Void Runners that separates it from other four-player co-op shooters.

## The Run Structure: Three Sectors, Three Guardians

The one piece of macro structure the store page does confirm is the shape of a run: three sectors, each four jumps deep, ending at a guardian. There are three guardians in total, one per sector.

That tells you something useful about pacing even without any numbers attached. A run is finite and readable. Twelve jumps total, broken into three legs of four, with a boss at the end of each leg instead of only at the very end. That structure suggests each sector builds toward a discrete test rather than a single long attrition slog.

What it does not tell you:

- How long a jump takes
- Whether jumps are chosen, random, or fixed
- Whether guardians scale with crew size
- Whether a run can be paused or saved mid-sector

None of that is documented. The store page describes the skeleton of a run but not its texture. You can verify the structure yourself once the game releases simply by playing through a run and counting jumps to the first guardian.

## Playing With One to Four People

The description confirms one to four pilots on one couch or online. That range matters because the beam web scales with crew size:

- **One player:** no beam partner, so the linking mechanic presumably works differently or not at all for solo play. The store page does not explain how solo play handles beams.
- **Two players:** one beam, one relationship to manage. The most legible version of the mechanic.
- **Three players:** the geometry gets interesting, since links can form chains or triangles depending on implementation, which the store page does not specify.
- **Four players:** the most chaotic and, presumably, the most rewarding version of the spacing puzzle.

Because the store page lists online play alongside couch co-op, assume you will not need to gather everyone in one room. Still, the emphasis on physical positioning suggests local play will feel different from online play, since you can see your partner's screen and body language on a couch in a way you cannot over voice chat.

Reviving crew from escape pods is explicitly advertised, which means death is not necessarily the end of a player's run. That is a meaningful design choice for a beam-link game: losing a pilot changes the web itself, so recovery has a structural cost, not just a numeric one.

## Building a Salvaged Ship

The store page mentions "building a salvaged ship," which ties the theme together. You are not choosing a loadout from a clean menu; you are assembling something from what you find. That framing is consistent with the roguelike genre, where runs are defined by improvised combinations rather than fixed builds.

What is not public: any part names, any upgrade paths, any indication of whether ship building happens mid-run, between jumps, or between sectors. There is no published code system, no unlock list, and no currency. If a source claims otherwise, it is not drawing on developer documentation.

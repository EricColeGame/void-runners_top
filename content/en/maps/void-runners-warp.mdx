"Void Runners" is an easy name to confuse with something else. There is a stylish action game about skating across a broken world, a co-op spaceship sim, a puzzle-driven dungeon crawler, and a long-running tabletop space fantasy — and none of them are this. The Void Runners on Steam (app 5334520) is a separate, smaller, and currently unreleased project from developer and publisher Aaron Reisman. It is a twin-stick roguelike for one to four players, it is built around beams that connect your ships, and as of this writing it has not shipped.

That last point matters more than usual. ## The core idea: your position is the weapon

Most twin-stick shooters ask one question: where do you aim? Void Runners asks a different one. Each pair of ships is joined by a beam, and that beam shreds whatever it sweeps. You are not just dodging bullets and firing back — you are dragging a damaging line across the screen, and the line only exists because two players (or two of your own hands) are in specific places.

That single design choice cascades into everything else the store description mentions:

- **Spreading out** widens what the beam can reach and sweeps across more of the arena at once.
- **Swinging the web** implies rotating that line, so one player orbiting another turns a static tether into a cutting arc.
- **Crossfire** suggests overlapping beams from multiple ship pairs, which is where a four-player crew gets value that a solo pilot cannot replicate.
- **Dodging** stays central because a wide formation is also a big target.

The practical takeaway, even before release: coordination beats individual skill. A crew that flies in a tight, predictable clump wastes the beam. A crew that drifts apart without a plan sweeps empty space and eats damage. The interesting decisions happen in the space between those extremes.

- Do I have one to three people who will sit on the same couch, or play online on a schedule?
- Does my group enjoy positional, coordination-heavy play, or do we prefer independent power fantasy?
- Am I comfortable buying into a mechanically unusual roguelike before the meta-progression is public?

If the answers line up, the game is worth following. Nothing here guarantees a good time — the game has not been played by the public, and a concept that sounds great on paper can land differently in the hands.

What can be said with confidence is that the beam-linking idea is specific. It is not a generic twin-stick shooter with a co-op label slapped on. The design forces players to negotiate space with each other continuously, and that is a real, verifiable commitment in the store description rather than marketing filler.

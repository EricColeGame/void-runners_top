## What Void Runners Actually Is

According to the official Steam listing, Void Runners is a twin-stick roguelike for one to four players, playable either on a single couch (local co-op) or online. It is a salvage-raid game: you and your crew fly through hostile space, and each pair of ships is joined by a beam that destroys anything it sweeps across.

That single mechanic reshapes everything else. In a normal twin-stick shooter, your position matters because of what you can shoot. In Void Runners, your position matters because of what your beam crosses — and the beam is a line between two moving players, not a barrel attached to your own hull. The store description explicitly frames the appeal as "spreading out and swinging the web," creating crossfire, dodging, and reviving crew from escape pods, all while building up a salvaged ship run by run.

A run is described as crossing three sectors, each four jumps deep, ending at a guardian. That is the structure the developer has committed to publicly. Because the game is unreleased, no ship parts, weapon stats, guardian names, or sector layouts have been published, and no code system has been mentioned at all — anything claiming otherwise is not backed by the official page.

## How the Beam Mechanic Changes Co-op Play

The most practical thing a new player can understand before launch is the geometry problem the game creates. With one player, a beam is simply a sweeping weapon. With two or more, the beam becomes a relationship.

| Players | Beam pairs | Practical consequence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | 1 (a self-linked sweep) | Positioning is a solo aiming problem |
| 2 | 1 | Every enemy you kill is a shared decision about spacing |
| 3 | 3 | Cutting one beam off to dodge weakens two others |
| 4 | 6 | The web is dense but fragile; one bad drift tangles everyone |

The table above is reasoning from the advertised "each pair of ships is joined by a beam," not from a published rules document. The counts follow from pairing arithmetic, and they illustrate why more players is not automatically easier. A four-player crew has six simultaneous cutting lines going for it and six ways to accidentally pull a teammate out of position.

This is also why "spreading out and swinging the web" is the phrase to internalize. Spreading out lengthens and angles each beam through more of the screen. Swinging means rotating that line by flying, which turns movement itself into the attack. In practice, the interesting failure state in a game like this is not running out of bullets — it is two players drifting so that their beam points at empty space while a third player gets surrounded.

Because the game is not out, nobody outside the developer has played a finished build, so any claim about optimal formations, damage numbers, or which beam angles are strongest is speculative. Treat third-party guide content promising specific combos as observations about trailers, not as developer documentation, unless the official page says so.
